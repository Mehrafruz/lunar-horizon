The problem

At the lunar south pole, even a small change in timing can change a mission

  • At 84°S, the Sun never rises more than about 8° above the horizon.
  • Nearby ridges can block sunlight for hours or even days.
  • Earth stays in roughly the same part of the sky, but lunar libration shifts it by about 7° — enough to disappear behind the terrain.

A lander needs sunlight for power and a clear line of sight to Earth for direct communication. Ideally, both from touchdown.

Data and method

Real lunar terrain. Real astronomical positions.

1 · Celestial positions. JPL DE421 ephemeris: Sun and Earth every 3 hours, Nov 2026 – Dec 2030.

2 · Terrain analysis. NASA LRO LOLA elevation model, 240 m: local horizon in 1,440 directions for 7 polar sites, Moon's curvature included.

3 · 3D visualization. 38 × 38 km around the selected site, with sunlight and shadows at the chosen time.

4 · Implementation. Python data pipeline with tests; front end in HTML and JavaScript.

Visibility uses the horizon out to the edge of the LOLA map (75°S); the 3D scene shows only 38 × 38 km and is for illustration.5 / 7

Checked on a smooth Moon: this validates the Sun and Earth directions, not the terrain. The constant +0.0055° in the Sun's azimuth is aberration of light, which Horizons applies.

Known limitations
  • Each site is a single point, not a region: at the Bussey 2010 point our model gives 60% Sun; a ridge pixel 2.1 km away gives 86%.
  • The 240 m terrain cannot resolve ridges under ~500 m; rise and set times may be off by several hours.
  • Line of sight only: no ground stations, relay satellites, slopes or thermal limits.
  • Sites north of 75°S and your own points use a smooth Moon; positions every 3 hours.
Validated within the tested conditions — not a flight-certified mission-planning tool.6 / 7
